The Butterfly Effect

Father's Day

Kendall Evans · June 21, 2026

Father's Day used to be a work day.

Busy shifts. Packed restaurants. Constant motion from open to close.

No space to stop. Because stopping meant feeling everything.

It was just another shift back then.

It doesn't feel like that anymore.

Some places don't just hold memories of work.

They hold a version of life that doesn't exist anymore.

The version where there was a home to return to.

People waiting. Something stable to land on after exhaustion.

That version is gone.

Grief doesn't always look like sadness.

Sometimes it looks like distance.

Disconnection from things that used to feel normal.

Even things that used to be survival.

Serving. Holidays. Full rooms of families.

Smiling like it was nothing.

It's not that it can't be done.

It's that something in me doesn't connect to it the same way anymore.

Because it pulls too much with it.

Memories of coming home.

A house that felt like safety.

A life that had rhythm. Structure. Familiarity.

And when that breaks, even normal things stop feeling normal.

Loss changes everything.

And everything else shifts with it.

Family. Distance. Silence.

At some point, absence stops feeling like a moment.

It becomes the pattern you live inside.

Your mind still tries to make sense of it.

Because not understanding it feels worse.

So meaning gets built in pieces.

Even when it's messy. Even when it changes later.

Life doesn't slow down for grief.

It still asks for movement. Stability. Answers.

Even when nothing inside is caught up yet.

Grief doesn't follow timing.

It overlaps everything.

Work. People. Memory. Safety.

It changes all of it.

There was a point where I was just trying to survive day to day.

The same situation I was left to face from the ground up once again without the blink of an eye.

No stable home.

No predictability.

Just moving through whatever part of the struggle came next.

But back then was a different story. I wasn't home anymore. I didn't realize the world was as dangerous as it really is.

It was the scariest, loneliest thing to experience alone.

Regardless of times when I wasn't.

It was a constant cycle that just kept repeating and never stopped. Like a nightmare.

Never knowing where I would end up.

Never fully grounded.

During that time, I was exposed to situations I had never experienced before.

Things that felt unsafe in ways I didn't yet know how to name.

I didn't have language for it then.

I had never lived like that before.

And I was completely on my own in it.

There was still a concept of "home" in my mind.

But it wasn't something I could return to.

It was something I was holding onto while everything else kept shifting.

That split slowly started to break something internally.

Because when there's no consistent safety, your nervous system stops resetting.

It stops knowing what normal is supposed to feel like.

That's where things started to change.

Not all at once.

But slowly.

Socially. Emotionally. Physically.

Talking to people became hard.

Being in public became overwhelming.

Simple interactions started to feel like too much.

Eventually, even basic communication felt like something my body resisted.

That wasn't who I used to be.

It was what I became to survive what was happening.

And it stayed with me long after.

After my dad died, everything didn't settle.

It shifted again.

I had made it through one kind of instability.

But I was pushed back into another.

Still without real grounding.

Still trying to function inside emotional chaos while appearing like I was fine.

So instead of healing, it layered.

And that's part of what the doctor later saw when he stopped and questioned what had been diagnosed.

"Who diagnosed you?"

And then:

"You're not bipolar."

"You have severe PTSD."

For the first time, there was language that actually matched what my body had been living through.

In the middle of all of it, life still kept asking for forward motion.

Work. Direction. Stability.

"Figure it out."

Even when nothing inside was stable enough to support that yet.

And grief doesn't care about timing.

It doesn't move on schedule.

It moves when it can.

Under everything is still the idea of home.

Not a place.

A feeling.

Where things made sense.

Where love felt present.

Where returning didn't feel like starting over emotionally every time.

That version doesn't fully come back.

So it becomes something internal instead.

Something carried.

There's pressure to treat survival like success.

To act like getting through it quickly is the goal.

But grief doesn't care about speed.

It cares about truth.

Still, something remains.

Not going back.

But the belief that stability can exist again in a different form.

Something that doesn't erase what happened.

But can hold it without breaking under it.

Something that slowly becomes less about surviving.

And more about living again.
